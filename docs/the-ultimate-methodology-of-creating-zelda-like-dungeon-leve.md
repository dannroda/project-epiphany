### The Ultimate Methodology of creating Zelda Dungeon Level

### Preface

The Legend of Zelda series is hailed as the most brilliant jewel in gaming history’s crown. Over the course of more than thirty years, each entry in The Legend of Zelda has courageously innovated and broken conventions while preserving its core experience — the constant sense of novelty in exploration.

A year ago, the author first played The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild and was deeply captivated by the “hakoniwa” level design of the shrines and Divine Beasts. Since then, over the past year, the author has gradually completed all major titles in the series, witnessing the development and evolution of the series’ hakoniwa design history. Now the time has come, and the author hopes to connect the hakoniwa design across the entire Legend of Zelda series and share with you the design intricacies within.

Please note that this article is not a deconstruction, but rather starts from the simplest form of hakoniwa and gradually expands following the designers’ thinking. In other words, it feels more like “if you were a Zelda designer, how would you step by step arrive at where we are today.” The author believes that rather than directly telling everyone the so-called solution, it’s better to walk through the problem-solving process together, which is…

The evolution of hakoniwa design that exists nowhere else in gaming history.

### Part 1: The Birth of Lock and Key

The primary hakoniwa in The Legend of Zelda series are various mazes. In the series’ early works, these mazes mostly took the form of dungeons — exploring and advancing through small rooms one by one. When designing dungeons, a linear structure is the most fundamental design approach.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*Anqsl1_mng6nWMda_7ie3Q.png)

While there’s continuous positive feedback from progression, it doesn’t really embody the “sense of freedom in exploration”… So how about adding some branch rooms alongside the critical path of the maze?

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*s4hVLZUr1_IxkJSuFxOLuA.png)

Although branch rooms can give players a sense of freedom in choosing which path to take first, they’re essentially meaningless (though this can be remedied by placing small rewards at the ends of branch rooms). Players have no necessary reason to visit branch rooms, which means the hakoniwa has added many parts that may not be experienced by players, wasting game development resources.

How can we make exploring branch rooms a necessary action?

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*q5epKmpOremDjMxCFcmPew.png)

Thus, the lock-and-key structure was born. If we place a lock at the end of Room 1 and put the key in branch Room 4, then exploring the branch room becomes a necessary action. At the same time, the lock and key provide a kind of “false sense of exploration freedom,” and this pseudo-freedom can be further enhanced.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*VAB_1U4yAZ0MnMo_WeaAwA.png)

When a player holds one key while being shown three locked rooms simultaneously, this gives meaning to choice. This meaning is reflected in the fact that choice carries risk (that is, “opportunity cost”) — if you choose one door, you temporarily lose the opportunity to open the other two doors. The designer creates an illusion of freedom, then limits this freedom through “meaningful choices,” making each player action valuable.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*wNlTEsNVuEuwJ6bfvy2Z4Q.png)

We can also consider the opposite situation: when players simultaneously hold multiple keys, the pseudo-freedom of exploration is also enhanced. In such cases, players can choose which path to explore first, and this choice also carries the cost of consuming keys.

Therefore, based on the lock-and-key structure, designers have a starting point to create meaningful and intricate hakoniwa levels. Simply by adjusting the positions, quantities, and order of contact with players for keys and locks, the prototype of hakoniwa with different complexities and difficulties takes shape.

…But perhaps now we’ve created in one go a maze with 20 rooms using the lock-and-key structure, and a new problem has come knocking: exploring this massive maze that might take an hour to complete is truly tedious!

### Part 2: Establishing Short-Term Goals

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*U5ZdZkkkox0XUMkMa1jO3Q.png)

During the hakoniwa experience, players currently form only one goal — reaching the end from the starting point. This is a long-term goal, so players may very likely give up exploration midway due to the inability to gain short-term positive feedback. Therefore, breaking down the long-term goal into several short-term goals is what maintains players’ motivation to continue playing.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*6Sj7O5_SJ_uGyqsaDfFTSQ.png)

Traditional dungeons in The Legend of Zelda series all feature several short-term goals. Players need to first find the map and compass, which gives players a basic understanding of the dungeon’s structure; subsequently, players will obtain the key item in the dungeon or defeat a mini-boss; finally, players must find the boss key in the dungeon and use it to open the door to the boss room.

To summarize, these are the short-term goals that appear in hakoniwa and their sequence:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*y45t3FanT4c40-llTqJ5dg.png)

Every hakoniwa follows this process. Naturally, when players enter a new hakoniwa, they will also know the entire hakoniwa’s process structure, as well as what they should do first and what to do later. The establishment of short-term goals realizes this process structure, on one hand shaping the emotional curve of players experiencing the hakoniwa, and on the other hand helping players build a cognitive framework for all hakoniwa in their minds.

### Part 3: Spatial Awareness

Now, we have already achieved a sense of exploration in the hakoniwa through the lock-and-key structure and short-term goals. This is already sufficient… In the 1980s and 1990s, perhaps this content was already the limit of what could be loaded onto game cartridges. However, The Legend of Zelda never stops at sufficient. Design, so to speak, is “dancing in the shackles of limitations.” With the premise of being unable to expand new gameplay, the only thing left to surpass is — the hakoniwa space itself.

Thus, testing players’ spatial awareness became part of the DNA of The Legend of Zelda series’ hakoniwa.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*k0cXWEDS1SgGvqqS0xzQrQ.png)

Rooms in hakoniwa don’t necessarily have only one layer. In the Eastern Palace of The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past, rooms are divided into upper and lower levels: initially, players can only explore the upper level, while the large treasure chest on the lower level becomes an excellent lure. This type of room is equivalent to superimposing two rooms together, deepening the hakoniwa’s complexity. Because players cannot jump directly from the upper level to the lower level or climb from the lower level to the upper level, it creates an attraction of “you can see it but can’t touch it.”

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*7lzojTS5aZj93BcW9loOUw.png)

Another hakoniwa in this game, the Tower of Hera, also demonstrates the designers’ exploration of spatial awareness. This hakoniwa is divided into multiple floors, with holes in some rooms; if you accidentally fall through a hole, you’ll drop onto the floor below. On the 3rd floor of the Tower of Hera, there’s a large treasure chest containing a key item, but the chest is surrounded by holes, meaning players cannot directly reach the ground where the chest is located on the 3rd floor. Thus, the correct route is to first go up to the 4th floor, find the corresponding position and jump into the hole, thereby falling directly to the corresponding ground on the 3rd floor to open the large treasure chest.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*iB4c9-_r0vGlgQrdyxjclg.png)

Let’s look at another example, which involves a level mechanism. Raised blue walls appear in the hakoniwa blocking the player’s path. Players then need to go to a specific room and strike the crystal orb inside that room, which will toggle the raised/lowered state of all blue walls throughout the entire hakoniwa. After this, players can return to the first room and explore the routes previously blocked by the blue walls.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*ngv-2NAWya_R9uCV_sayuw.png)

In reality, the designers made this mechanism even more complex. The hakoniwa doesn’t only have blue walls; there’s also a type of orange wall. In this example, players try to jump into the hole in the center of the first room, but currently the blue walls are raised and the orange walls are lowered. So, after players reach the specific room and strike the crystal orb, the orange walls will rise while the blue walls will lower. Returning to the first room the same way, players discover they cannot push the block on the right side, and the orange walls also prevent players from reaching the left side of the room to try pushing the block on the left… What a dilemma! The only solution is: take a long detour back to the left side of that room, finally push the block into the hole, and also gain the opportunity to jump into the hole themselves.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*1yTJLOORtbkgbXvy9F-13Q.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*ccKQCSUp_Yc34NODzV6Hyg.png)

The blue-orange wall mechanism requires players to have extremely strong spatial awareness of the entire hakoniwa, rather than just focusing on a single room. In short, players can actively switch the overall structure of the hakoniwa. The GMTK video series calls this type of hakoniwa a “puzzle box,” which follows several principles (for details, please watch “How Zelda’s Puzzle Box Dungeons Work”):

The switch is fixed in one room

Toggling the switch will open some paths in the hakoniwa while closing other paths

Players can toggle the switch state back and forth

The switch toggle takes effect throughout the entire hakoniwa

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*-08BrlPfIA2qPOzMUpgd7Q.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*6nYViw7OR4dz_vWoSPe-ug.png)

The Water Temple in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time is a prime example of “puzzle box” hakoniwa: the water level of the entire level is divided into low, medium, and high settings, and the switches to adjust the water level are distributed across three different rooms; thus, players need to run to these three rooms multiple times to play the Ocarina of Time, toggling the water level of the entire level back and forth to advance exploration.

After looking at these cases, please consider what the essence of emphasizing spatial awareness in hakoniwa is. That’s right, we want players to visit the rooms we’ve designed multiple times. In a linear hakoniwa, each room is visited only once, but if the test of spatial awareness is embedded, players must backtrack, thus the number of room visits greatly increases.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*ELPpMPk2Oxng4LqT98LkGA.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*cetCflMbOesgS0v78q94iA.png)

But is a hakoniwa with maxed-out spatial awareness really good? Perhaps hardcore players will marvel at the process of solving such hakoniwa, but after Eiji Aonuma designed the Water Temple, he was endlessly criticized by players at the time. Testing players’ spatial awareness means increasing the spatial complexity of the entire hakoniwa, and players can easily get disoriented within the hakoniwa.

So, what methods can be used to appropriately reduce the spatial complexity of hakoniwa?

### Part 4: Spatial Complexity

Before discussing methods to reduce spatial complexity, everyone needs to first understand a concept — the mental map. When players explore a hakoniwa, they gradually remember the general structure of the hakoniwa in their minds. This vague memory is the player’s mental map.

The essence of reducing spatial complexity is making it easier for players to form a mental map of the hakoniwa!

After the release of The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past, designers realized the problem: the dungeons we designed were far too intricate and complex! This problem didn’t only appear in hakoniwa that emphasized spatial awareness, but would occur whenever the number of rooms in a hakoniwa increased. Thus, designers began a long exploration of reducing spatial complexity…

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*IRi8bTisWq2Jr0qkOUTafg.png)

The simplest method is to create a central hub room: define one room in the hakoniwa as the central large room, and have all routes converge in this room. As long as players have an impression of this hub room, they can use it as a reference point to visualize the distance of each room relative to the hub room in their minds, thereby easily constructing a mental map.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*loeCpdMiTDSxfhhVmhuB0g.png)

The Legend of Zelda: Twilight Princess further developed the hub room: when players arrive at the hub room, they discover the door directly ahead is blocked, so they look to the left and right sides and find that one side’s door is also locked. Thus, the player’s gameplay route becomes “first explore room group 1 — obtain key — then explore room group 2 — obtain key item — finally explore room 2 — reach the end,” which is “sides first, center last.”

The “sides first, center last” hub room is so useful that it became a formula. Why do you think nearly every hakoniwa in The Legend of Zelda: Twilight Princess uses this formula without getting tired of it?

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*fUMZJoWO42IDU1Gi4UQeWA.png)

That’s right, its essence is a linear progression, only it cleverly disguises this to create a “general — specific — general” exploration flow.

Next, let’s look at another structure based on hub rooms.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*E_v7ja9mmOA1OzoGkTOXSQ.png)

The Forest Temple in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time uses a “multi-layered concentric circle” hub room: players first start from the hub room and explore outward in a circle, going through rooms 1, 2, and 3 before returning to the hub room; then, players set out from the hub room again to explore a further outer circle, going through rooms 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 before returning to the hub room, and finally activating the elevator in the hub room to go downstairs and fight the boss.

The “multi-layered concentric circle” allows players to occasionally see, or even briefly return to, inner layer rooms while exploring outer layer rooms, deepening the connections between rooms.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*TGeftd7jR8HTM0famJltpg.png)

An example you’re probably more familiar with is the temples in The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom. The Water Temple and Lightning Temple also use hub rooms, but they have a “central radiating” structure. Players can freely choose the order in which to visit rooms 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, but these rooms all connect to a hub room located in the center.

Wait a minute, there don’t seem to be hub rooms in The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild… How did it manage to reduce spatial complexity?

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*dljhed2azslzEPyhGkYn6A.png)

That’s right, by making the hakoniwa itself into an easily recognizable shape. When exploring inside the Divine Beasts in The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, players naturally form mental maps based on their position relative to the Divine Beast (I’m currently at a certain part of the Divine Beast). The hakoniwa in The Legend of Zelda: Link’s Awakening also uses this technique, where we can see key-shaped and turtle-shaped hakoniwa (perhaps these were the prototypes for the Divine Beasts…).

### Part 5: Room Content

Next, we can turn our attention to the content of each room.

The player’s action process is “thinking — doing.” Different degrees of emphasis on these two aspects will give players different forms of internal reward. The Eureka moment (aha moment) we often mention when solving puzzles is the pleasure produced after thinking; while after completing various challenges that test the player’s action abilities, what the player gains is a sense of achievement. In short, it goes like this: thinking — Eureka moment — doing — sense of achievement.

Therefore, room content can also focus on either thinking or doing.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*0a3JJsv_Vp0KuS6k6r8flQ.png)

The doing part includes combat and dynamic elements (such as parkour on moving platforms), while the thinking part includes the previously mentioned spatial awareness and puzzle gameplay.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*Pm2eBRALsgnRf3fd1e_V0w.png)

In early The Legend of Zelda series games, all hakoniwa shared a common set of components (blue and orange walls, various trap mechanisms, etc.). In each room, players basically encountered various combinations of enemies and dynamic elements, heavily emphasizing the doing part, with essentially no puzzle gameplay created specifically for a single hakoniwa. Therefore, to differentiate between hakoniwa, one could only rely on spatial awareness — that is, how to arrange the overall spatial planning of the hakoniwa.

This gave birth to many spatial awareness-based gameplay pioneers:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*I5XNHQRFz5x3R7wp-tBThQ.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*xjGxCBf9qVM-h135CTiztA.png)

The Swamp Palace is one of the pioneers of switching water levels

However, by the era of The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, cartridge capacity had expanded, and designers could finally fill the hakoniwa with more unique and interesting gameplay. So they made the following changes:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*HAohi2J2XRAeFsdiqGRIbA.png)

In this game, the content of hakoniwa rooms included combat, spatial awareness, dynamic elements, puzzles, and time challenges, with these contents appearing in alternation without primary or secondary distinction. During the exploration of room after room, players could clearly notice — the content of each room was novel.

In an interview before this game’s release, Shigeru Miyamoto introduced his hakoniwa design philosophy, which was “an exploration experience like Disneyland.” Each hakoniwa is a theme, and under this theme are many different rooms and gameplay experiences, with each room bringing players a novel experience.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*8WVOziSSk49Sg_89oAdJmA.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*3uU_Mhr32RkndPkFOHv7nw.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*eCpre-UsWwtaAj2TXQiR6g.png)

However, starting with The Legend of Zelda: The Wind Waker, Eiji Aonuma gradually established a paradigm for the series’ hakoniwa, which wasn’t broken and reconstructed until The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*thMJKlNN5uBtHXmaWpQ-tQ.png)

This paradigm is to place each hakoniwa’s distinctive feature in one or two core puzzles. These puzzles combine both doing and thinking elements, and as players delve deeper into the hakoniwa, they become increasingly comprehensive and difficult. Pure doing parts like combat became the lubricant and transition between puzzles.

How do you determine each hakoniwa’s core puzzles, or core gameplay?

Use key items!

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*G--G6ws9CIunzQNnQ6-wxg.png)

When discussing short-term goals earlier, we mentioned that each hakoniwa gives players a key item along the way, and this key item can help players unlock previously impassable routes. For example, if the key item you obtain is a bomb, you can backtrack and blow up cracked rocks to open new paths.

The key item is a key that can unlock multiple locks. The moment players obtain the key item, they shift from passive to active, completely taking control of exploring the dungeon. This is an experiential shift of “starting to turn the tables.”

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*NkUupVxjlcZzhxJlhkp84Q.png)

In that case, why not directly design each hakoniwa’s core gameplay around the key item? This can both strengthen the moment of this experiential shift and give the item more depth of playability.

Now, we can outline a most basic hakoniwa gameplay flow. First, design a universal gameplay set for players to experience in the first few rooms after just entering the hakoniwa. Then, after players obtain the key item in the hakoniwa, design core gameplay or puzzles triggered by this key item and place them in subsequent rooms (of course, they can also be placed in previous rooms as hints and bait).

Below is a more detailed layout approach for gameplay after obtaining the key item:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*SUjVmEVwMtFPyBM9p_UqvQ.png)

The layout of core gameplay needs to follow two principles: teaching the player and exploring depth. I won’t elaborate in detail here on the “introduction-development-twist-conclusion” structure and puzzle design methodology.

Next, let’s temporarily switch perspectives and see how designers create interesting key items.

### Part 6: Character Abilities

In adventure games, character abilities can be divided into the following types:

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*jOxmzltHgOFRVoX_Zk3cZQ.png)

Inherent character abilities are the basic actions a character can perform, including walking, running, and sprinting. The Legend of Zelda series has added actions like “pick up/put down items” and “push/pull items” to expand the universal gameplay toolkit (such as pushing boxes and transporting balls). In The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, to accommodate open-world exploration, actions like “climbing” and “gliding” were also incorporated into inherent character abilities.

Special character abilities are “actions that ordinary humans cannot perform alone,” which include using key items such as bombs, hookshots, and magnets. In series entries like The Legend of Zelda: The Minish Cap, special character abilities also include the game’s core featured gameplay, such as shrinking. Link can shrink to the size of a bean and enter small holes that cannot be explored at normal size, discovering entirely different worlds within.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*DBw_217foCk0HOyCVtjrmQ.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*IsQsnZq7sfNb-Z7JRv9lEg.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*ioIyTwTtV5g7fwxyIS6Whw.png)

The character can shrink their body to infiltrate small holes or the inside of robots

Additionally, The Legend of Zelda: The Wind Waker and The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom also feature companion systems, where players can use companions’ abilities and even temporarily control companions to help them explore hakoniwa.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*JcmdVaNhoz89_h0IpyoxUg.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*bqI7i7QogtEFI_70Ndfd3w.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*KifcHZ_m-g9REn_KH-ZdTg.png)

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*4uWs-Ju0AwPnQzUIVXL5Cw.png)

Re-examining the room content of The Legend of Zelda hakoniwa from the perspective of character abilities, we can use “obtaining key items” as a dividing point: before this, room content includes inherent ability gameplay sets (combat and dynamic elements), existing item ability gameplay sets (key items obtained from previous dungeons or the overworld), and special ability gameplay sets (core gameplay mechanics like shrinking); after this, room content is primarily designed around key item ability gameplay sets. At the same time, key item ability gameplay sets can also combine with the previous three gameplay sets to create more comprehensive challenges or puzzles.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*knUY08ase9DbhAdRfn2Tzw.png)

Let’s start with the inherent ability gameplay set. It includes three aspects: combat, dynamic elements, and environmental object puzzles.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*HaHM83VLRJZHzARdf4Wwsg.png)

Among these, particular attention should be paid to environmental object puzzles. This is a way for players to solve puzzles using only inherent character abilities when they don’t have key items. However, inherent abilities are necessarily limited, so sometimes rooms will contain objects that players can interact with using inherent abilities, and these objects embody certain mechanisms as part of the puzzle-solving process.

A very typical example is the bomb flower.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*KrT3cH-Urt861jLCwz8bOg.png)

Bomb flowers generally appear in the progression before players have obtained the bomb as a key item. They serve the function of bombs, with designers placing them near puzzles that require bombs to solve.

It can be observed that environmental object puzzles actually blur the boundary between inherent character abilities and key item abilities, but they have one limitation — the positions of objects are all fixed in the scene by designers. When players truly obtain bombs, they gain the freedom to use this item, able to use it anytime and anywhere without depending on the limitations of scene placement.

So, returning to the original question, how can key items be designed to be interesting?

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*H8KiBpQbLOgCnZ2R1Jek8A.png)

The many hakoniwa in The Legend of Zelda: Twilight Princess are memorable because of the key items within them. These key items not only possess lock-and-key puzzle-solving properties but also have a pure operational joy. In the Arbiters Grounds, players obtain the Spinner, which can not only embed into certain floors to rotate and solve puzzles but can also slot into rails to move quickly, providing a roller coaster-like thrill. Similarly, in Goron Mines, players can equip the Iron Boots, using greater gravity to activate certain pressure switches; but surprisingly, the Iron Boots can magnetically attach players to specific walls, even allowing them to walk upside down on ceilings!

The multifunctionality of key items makes them more interesting, and hakoniwa that utilize these multifunctional items become highly memorable.

However, there are points to be mindful of when designing key items.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*F2nepJF9V9dnSWvStjYHsA.png)

As shown in the image, many key items in The Legend of Zelda: Twilight Princess only have opportunities to be used in specific hakoniwa, with no further use in the game’s subsequent progression. In contrast, key items in The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild can be used frequently by players, whether in the overworld or in hakoniwa.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*DhAhNPA8R8DqMwHt2jEFVg.png)

Therefore, when designing special character abilities including key items, it’s best to consider whether they can integrate with other systems in the game.

### Part 7: Excellent Hakoniwa Case Studies

In this section, I will share some interesting tidbits about hakoniwa design that I personally find noteworthy.

First, let’s look at some supplementary case studies regarding short-term goals.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*aCbySiqEauN6tILupgVa_A.png)

The temples in The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom typically contain 4–5 puzzles that need to be solved, with the central room displaying the number of puzzles the player has already completed. The Forest Temple in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time also visualizes player progress: the central room has 4 torches, and players need to find and defeat 4 corresponding Poe ghosts throughout the hakoniwa, causing the torches in the central room to light up one by one.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*b_MD-L9fQHYrVdvoivRKWQ.png)

Rescuing characters is a common method for splitting up short-term goals. When players enter the Fire Temple in The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time, they’re told they need to explore the hakoniwa and rescue several imprisoned Gorons scattered throughout; each time a player rescues a Goron (completing a short-term goal), they receive a hint for the dungeon. The Forest Temple in The Legend of Zelda: Twilight Princess takes this method even further: players need to rescue several monkeys, who at the end act as a rope, all grabbing onto the player together and swinging them to the other side of a previously unreachable cliff.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*FLKHwTqYr7O0WBxn3P3yrA.png)

Transporting objects is a puzzle type frequently used throughout The Legend of Zelda series: players need to physically carry an object and transport it to a specific location. The Eagle’s Tower in The Legend of Zelda: Link’s Awakening is a classic example: players need to carry a large iron ball and transport it to the four corners of the tower’s second floor, using it to smash the 4 supporting pillars. As a result, the Eagle’s Tower collapses, merging the fourth and third floors into one.

The Temple of Time in The Legend of Zelda: Twilight Princess is another case I really enjoy: players need to explore a linear hakoniwa section, reach the deepest part of the hakoniwa, and obtain the key item, the Dominion Rod. Next, players discover they can use the rod to control a nearby large statue and need to transport this statue back to the entrance of the hakoniwa. In other words, players now need to traverse the hakoniwa in reverse, but this time they must account for the statue as an additional factor.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*31X1Tr_mWXmWK3a19IQZQA.png)

As just mentioned, The Legend of Zelda: The Minish Cap’s core mechanic of shrinking the player is highly successful because its usage frequency remains high throughout the entire game. In the overworld, players can discover many holes that require shrinking to enter, and Hyrule Town’s design perfectly demonstrates the versatility of this shrinking mechanic. Although it’s a town meant for rest and resupply, Hyrule Town presents many pathways that can be explored while shrunk, and these pathways act as bait attracting players. Thus, driven by various side quests, players can truly shrink and explore Hyrule Town from another dimension, gradually revealing the full scope of the town.

Similarly, this game’s hakoniwa also utilize the shrinking core mechanic. In the Fortress of Winds, players need to switch between normal and shrunk forms to explore the hakoniwa, and the boss at the end even requires players to simultaneously use both the shrinking ability and key items to defeat it… First disable the large robot, then shrink and drill into its body to wreak havoc!

Compared to this title, the core ability in The Legend of Zelda: Twilight Princess seems somewhat underwhelming. Players can transform into wolf form, but the usage scenarios and frequency of wolf form are not very high. Designers created several additional gameplay mechanics for wolf form, but didn’t organically integrate them into the game as a whole, making the use of this core ability feel rather disconnected.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*4GXSRR0AVjvtirvr59eK5A.png)

In The Legend of Zelda: Phantom Hourglass and The Legend of Zelda: Spirit Tracks, designers created a type of hakoniwa that requires multiple visits from players. Players can explore the Temple of the Ocean King and the Tower of Spirits early in the game’s progression, but can only explore three floors. After clearing other hakoniwa in the overworld, players can return here and continue exploring deeper. So, the overall game flow becomes: Temple of the Ocean King / Tower of Spirits — Overworld Hakoniwa 1 — Temple of the Ocean King / Tower of Spirits — Overworld Hakoniwa 2 — Temple of the Ocean King / Tower of Spirits — Overworld Hakoniwa 3 — Temple of the Ocean King / Tower of Spirits…

To make these repeatedly-visited hakoniwa distinctive, designers adopted different approaches in these two titles. Exploration of the Temple of the Ocean King is time-limited, starting with only 10 minutes, and each time players defeat a boss in another hakoniwa, the exploration time for the Temple of the Ocean King increases, allowing for deeper exploration.

The Tower of Spirits’ core gameplay is two-character puzzle-solving. Players can simultaneously control Link and Princess Zelda, who possesses Phantoms. On different floors of the Tower of Spirits, Princess Zelda can possess different types of Phantoms — some can cross spike traps, some can carry Link across lava, some have self-illuminating flame swords, and some can teleport within the hakoniwa.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*PjKb1oPbhLQVQc_h9CHihQ.png)

Although The Legend of Zelda series tends to separate the hakoniwa and overworld experiences, there are still some excellent hakoniwa that interact with the overworld. In Skull Woods from The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past, the forest’s surface and underground dungeon correspond vertically. Therefore, players need to travel back and forth between the surface and underground multiple times. Sometimes, falling through a hole on the surface can drop you into a previously unreachable room underground.

The Spirit Temple in The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom splits the hakoniwa into small factories located at 4 positions in the overworld. Players must sequentially transport components from the factories into the overworld, assembling and combining the parts into a large robot. After this, players need to pilot the robot through the overworld, defeat enemies, and reach the boss battle location situated at another spot in the overworld.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*xgWrljYFUys388mzn7u6nQ.png)

### Part 8: Breaking Paradigms — Breath of the Wild

At this point, many new players to the series might have a question: this is completely different from the Zelda I’ve played!

That’s right — “returning to the origin” and “breaking paradigms” were the core objectives for Hidemaro Fujibayashi when creating The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild. Over the years of development, The Legend of Zelda series had accumulated many design paradigms; these were like shackles, gradually constraining this great game series and making it difficult to move forward.

So, it was time to break those long-standing patterns of thinking, start from The Legend of Zelda’s origins, and be reborn from the ashes.

So, let me ask everyone: what is the core of The Legend of Zelda series’ hakoniwa? What things, what concepts do we need to pass down? And what things are constraining us that can be discarded?

Let’s see Hidemaro Fujibayashi’s answer.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*0iilYYTZJnAcgiUl02Lenw.png)

First, we must retain key items as special character abilities, but these key items need to be more deeply embedded in the open world. Magnesis, Cryonis, Stasis — these abilities can all be used in the open world at any time with good reason… Hmm, still doesn’t feel quite enough…

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*Te_roxLlM9dl7Amo1Ch_AA.png)

Thus, the 120 shrines scattered across the world were born. In each shrine, players can use these key items in a targeted manner. Their sequences are all short and sweet, because usage frequency matters more than individual duration.

Since we’ve gone this far, why not directly eliminate the paradigm of obtaining key items in large hakoniwa? By giving players all key items directly in the first 4 shrines at the very beginning of the game, with tutorials structured around introduction, development, twist, and conclusion, players obtain all key items in the early stage. This is a design optimization for open-world exploration.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*k_ZDkcpIHy-FZsSizRQolw.png)

So, in The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild’s large hakoniwa, what should we retain? The answer is spatial awareness, lock-and-key structure, and key item gameplay sets. However, they all need to be adjusted accordingly. For spatial awareness, designers created the Four Divine Beasts, a “puzzle box” type of hakoniwa, but one that doesn’t conform to our previous definition. Players can adjust the overall structure of the Divine Beast at any time, without having to go to a specific room to trigger it. In other words, the detour characteristic attached to spatial awareness has been eliminated.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*ID5nqVqVDyQ2cKVbE0PQ5Q.png)

The lock-and-key structure, as its name suggests, is a design approach that greatly restricts player movement, which actually contradicts this game’s sense of freedom based on an open world. Therefore, designers simplified the lock-and-key to its most basic form — 4–5 completely parallel rooms or puzzles. After solving these puzzles in any order, players can face the Boss. As for key item gameplay sets, designers no longer need to consider how to teach players step by step, but can directly increase the difficulty, creating puzzles that require multiple key item abilities working together.

But the emotional experience of “turning the tables” after obtaining a key item in hakoniwa is gone. If the entire hakoniwa only has 5 puzzles, the player’s experience will be quite bland. Is there a solution to this? Think about the sequence before entering the Divine Beast!

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*pBxRYlcrLU3GIBOL-_w5uQ.png)

Each Divine Beast has a different entry method, but they all share the same framework: you must first bring down the Divine Beast and paralyze it before you can enter and begin solving puzzles inside. The process of defeating the Divine Beast emphasizes action, while exploring the Divine Beast itself emphasizes thinking. At the moment when the Divine Beast is paralyzed, players experience a similar “turning the tables” emotional experience.

### Part 9: Building on the Past, Opening the Future — Tears of the Kingdom

Finally, at last, we arrive at The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom. This work reconstructs key items.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*UJozVHZp0qyIPZ_Q_6iDjg.png)

Ultrahand and Fuse are overpowered key item abilities that link to the entire Zonai Device system, and each Zonai Device can be considered a type of key item. These numerous devices are placed in shrines to be taught one by one.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*buhbVQKgmFJ2JUk_GbLZsg.png)

The power that The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom grants players is the ability to freely combine these Zonai Devices. Through Ultrahand, players can create custom devices in almost any form, incorporating multiple item abilities.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*pAiS38yYXx8AHH0N_52OSg.png)

Thus, when exploring temples and shrines, designers only define the starting point and destination, without restricting the process in between. Players can reach the destination from the starting point in different ways by creating different custom devices. This is an unimaginable emergent sense of freedom.

Wait a minute — with this approach, the degree of freedom is too high. This game abandoned large-scale hakoniwa emphasizing spatial awareness, but also added another constraint: Sage abilities.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*pC16FsTIQa_8mXJDsunvGQ.png)

Sage abilities solve multiple problems. First, they serve to some extent the role that key items played in previous series entries — one key for multiple locks. In the Fire Temple, Yunobo’s rolling charge skill can break through obstructing Marbled Rock Roasts and also adjust the direction of tracks. Second, it ultimately constrains players’ free-form puzzle solving. To enable Yunobo to charge into specific objects, designers created corresponding puzzles targeting this companion skill, such as building a ramp so he can charge up the slope and crash into objects at higher elevations.

![image](https://miro.medium.com/v2/resize:fit:1400/1*O6T6v9UqJOZ-UHb4AHShxg.png)

Similar to the previous game, the complete experience of large-scale hakoniwa in The Legend of Zelda: Tears of the Kingdom is also divided into two parts: reaching the temple and the temple itself. Because it lacks the shocking experience of bringing down massive Divine Beasts, this game can only achieve similar effects through other means, such as the Wind Temple and Water Temple’s sequences of launching skyward via trampolines and waterfall climbing, or the Fire Temple and Lightning Temple’s sequences of moving the Boss battle to earlier in the experience.

### Epilogue

With this, we have reviewed the thirty-plus years of hakoniwa development history in The Legend of Zelda series. What are your thoughts?

Starting from scratch, gradually adding paradigms, and finally breaking those paradigms to be reborn — it’s quite moving, isn’t it?

My first The Legend of Zelda game was The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild. It captivated me, amazed me, and began my pilgrimage through the entire Zelda series. When I traced back from its origins, I found echoes of Breath of the Wild in every single entry. Behind those vastly different styles of hakoniwa lies an unchanging core that has been passed down. Even though every The Legend of Zelda game innovates — horseback riding, sailing, railways, shrinking, transforming into a wolf, motion controls — they are all so different. Yet in the process of actually playing them, I discovered that nothing has truly changed. That core has always been there.

I witnessed Shigeru Miyamoto’s historic 2D and 3D Zelda games, witnessed Eiji Aonuma take up the mantle, struggle through challenges, and ultimately prove himself worthy as a Zelda producer, and witnessed Hidemaro Fujibayashi’s courage in breaking the conventions established by the Zelda series, grasping that core, and reshaping The Legend of Zelda. Now, looking back once more at The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild, its greatness is truly incredible.

Carrying on a legacy requires courage, abandoning it requires courage, and shattering all appearances to rebuild them requires incomparable courage. The Legend of Zelda series is gaming history’s unique, one-of-a-kind miracle that has constantly surpassed itself. That courage, through Link (the connection), has been passed into the hearts of every player, and every adventure we experience in Hyrule is, unchanging through the ages,

a hymn to courage.
